Every year, Pakistani families spend six figures in dollars sending a child abroad
for MBBS, and a meaningful number of those students come home with a degree they
cannot use.

Not because they were lazy. Because nobody put three specific facts in front of them
before they paid the first instalment.

## Gate one: the NOC, and it comes first

Since the 2024 academic session, a Pakistani student intending to study medicine or
dentistry abroad is required to obtain a **No Objection Certificate from PMDC before
enrolling**.

The order matters more than the document. Getting the NOC before you go is
administrative. Discovering you needed it in your third year is not fixable by paying
a fee, hiring a lawyer, or asking nicely. Students in that position can find their
degree simply will not be accepted for registration.

Eligibility for that pathway generally requires around **60% in FSc Pre-Medical or
A-Levels** and a valid **MDCAT** result.

Read that last part carefully, because it is the point most agents blur: **going
abroad does not get you around MDCAT.** If you did not sit it or did not pass it, you
have not solved a problem — you have postponed meeting it until after you have spent
six years and USD 40,000.

## Gate two: accreditation, and what actually counts

Here is the sentence you will hear: _"The university is WHO listed and NMC
approved."_

It sounds like a guarantee. It is not one, for two reasons.

A **WHO or directory listing** is a directory entry. It is not an accreditation
judgement about the quality of the programme, and it is not what governs your
registration in Pakistan.

**"NMC approved"** refers to India's regulator. It has no bearing on whether a
Pakistani graduate can register with PMDC. That phrase appears constantly in
marketing aimed at Pakistani students because the brochures were written for the
Indian market and never rewritten.

What you should actually check is **WFME recognition** of the accrediting agency, and
then PMDC's own current position on that institution — on the date you are applying,
not the date your cousin applied. These lists change. A university that worked for
someone three years ago may not work for you.

## Gate three: the exam nobody mentions

To practise medicine in Pakistan with a foreign degree, you must go through PMDC's
equivalence process and pass the required national licensing examination.

**A large share of foreign-qualified graduates do not pass on the first attempt.**

That is the number that should govern the whole decision, and it is the one you will
never see in a brochure — because it is the number that would stop the sale.

It does not mean the route is worthless. It means the exam is the actual finish line,
not the graduation ceremony. Students who understand that from year one, and study
for it throughout, pass at far better rates than students who treat it as a
formality to deal with after six years of getting by.

## So is MBBS abroad a bad idea?

No. It is a legitimate route for a specific student:

- Could not secure a Pakistani medical seat, often narrowly
- **Has** a valid MDCAT result and the required FSc marks
- **Has** personally verified the institution against PMDC's current position
- **Has** the NOC in hand before departure
- Is going in understanding that the licensing exam is the real goal

For that student, an English-medium MBBS at USD 3,500–6,000 a year in Uzbekistan or
a comparable destination can be a sound and life-changing decision.

For a student missing any of those five, our honest advice is usually to fix the gap
first — retake MDCAT, or look seriously at dentistry, allied health, pharmacy, or a
non-medical degree in the UK, Canada or Australia where the qualification is directly
usable.
